Issue 03

6 August 2026: Amazon’s $3tn Club and the Cash Flow Question

Published 6 August 2026 · The Market Loop · 6 min read

Amazon has now joined the $3 trillion club, helped by strong results, AWS growth and excitement around AI. But the part I found more useful was not just the valuation milestone. It was the cash flow side of the story. Amazon is growing quickly, but it is also spending heavily on the infrastructure needed for AI and cloud demand. That makes it a good example of why revenue growth and profit do not always tell the full story.

Market value $3tn
Net sales $200.6bn
AWS sales growth 37%
FCF outflow $7.6bn

This Week in Markets

Reuters reported that Amazon’s market value passed $3 trillion for the first time, helped by strong earnings and demand for AWS, its cloud computing business. Its shares rose about 5% to a record high of $285.01, and the stock was up more than 23% for the year at that point.

Amazon’s own second-quarter results also looked very strong. Net sales rose 20% year-on-year to $200.6 billion, while AWS sales rose 37% to $42.2 billion. Amazon also reported operating income of $27.5 billion.

At first, those numbers look almost completely positive. Revenue is growing, AWS is growing quickly and the market clearly liked the results. But the part that caught my attention was free cash flow.

Amazon said free cash flow decreased to an outflow of $7.6 billion for the trailing twelve months. The company said this was mainly because of a large increase in purchases of property and equipment, linked to investment in artificial intelligence.

That made the story feel more like a proper finance case study. Amazon is growing, but it is also spending heavily to build the infrastructure needed for that growth.

Deal / Company Story of the Week

Amazon is no longer just viewed as an online shopping company. A big part of its value now comes from AWS, because cloud computing is becoming even more important as companies use more AI.

If businesses want to run AI models, store data and use more computing power, they need infrastructure. That is where companies like Amazon can benefit. AWS gives Amazon a position in the background of the AI boom, even if customers do not always see it directly.

But the difficult part is that this infrastructure is expensive. Data centres, servers, chips and energy capacity require huge spending before the financial return is fully clear. This means Amazon has to invest first and hope that demand keeps growing strongly enough to justify the cost.

That is why I think this story is more than just “AI stock goes up”. The market is excited about Amazon’s growth, but investors will eventually want to know whether today’s spending creates enough future cash flow.

Finance Concept Explained: Free Cash Flow

The finance concept this week is free cash flow, often shortened to FCF.

A simple way to think about it is:

Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditure

Operating cash flow is the cash a company generates from its normal business activities. Capital expenditure, or capex, is money spent on long-term assets such as data centres, equipment, buildings, servers and infrastructure.

Free cash flow matters because it shows how much cash is left after a company has paid for the investment needed to maintain and grow the business. This is different from profit.

A company can report strong profit but still have weak or negative free cash flow if it is spending a lot on long-term assets. That is why Amazon is such a useful example. The company reported strong sales growth and operating income, but free cash flow was still negative because investment spending was so high.

This does not automatically mean Amazon is in a bad position. Sometimes weak free cash flow can be a warning sign, but sometimes it means a company is investing heavily for future growth. The key is understanding why the cash flow is weak.

In Amazon’s case, the pressure seems linked to heavy spending on AI and cloud infrastructure. So the question is not only whether Amazon can grow. It is whether that growth can eventually produce enough cash to justify the investment.

Amazon’s results also show why profit numbers need to be read carefully. The company said its second-quarter net income included a large non-operating gain, mainly from its investment in Anthropic.

That does not mean the result is fake or wrong. It just means some of the profit came from an investment gain, not only from normal day-to-day operations.

So the better finance question is not just “Did Amazon make a profit?” The better question is: how much recurring cash can Amazon generate after funding the investment needed for AI and cloud growth?

My Take

I found this story useful because it showed me the difference between growth, profit and cash flow.

Before looking at the details, it would be easy to see Amazon crossing $3 trillion and think the story is only about AI excitement. But the cash flow side makes it more serious. Amazon is not only benefiting from AI demand. It is also having to spend a huge amount to support that demand.

The main thing I learned is that profit is not always enough. A company can look strong on the income statement, but investors still need to check whether that profit is turning into cash.

For me, Amazon’s $3 trillion valuation shows how much investors believe in AWS and AI infrastructure. But the real test will be whether Amazon’s spending today leads to stronger free cash flow in the future.

That is why I think this story is not just about Amazon becoming bigger. It is about whether the company can turn AI growth into real financial returns.

I’m still learning, so if I’ve missed something important, I’d genuinely appreciate being corrected.

Issue 02

28 July 2026: Apple’s $5tn Valuation Moment

Published 28 July 2026 · The Market Loop · 5 min read

Apple reaching a $5 trillion valuation made me stop and think about how the stock market actually puts a value on a company. The number itself is obviously huge, but I think the more interesting part is what sits behind it. Apple is not being valued only because of what it sells today. Investors are also making a judgement about its brand, ecosystem, future earnings and how much confidence they have in the business long term.

Market value $5tn
Peak value $5.036tn
Share price $342.89
Concept Market cap

This Week in Markets

This week, Apple briefly passed a $5 trillion market value for the first time. Reuters reported that Apple became only the second company to reach that level, after Nvidia. At one point, Apple’s share price reached $342.89, giving it a market value of about $5.036 trillion.

That number is obviously huge, but I think the more useful part is understanding what it represents. A company’s market value is not just a random headline. It reflects what investors are willing to pay for the company’s shares at that moment.

With Apple, investors are not only thinking about the iPhone. They are also thinking about the brand, customer loyalty, services revenue, future product launches and how much cash the company can keep generating over time.

Apple has built an ecosystem where many customers use several Apple products together. That probably makes the business feel more reliable than a company that depends on only one product or one trend.

Deal / Company Story of the Week

Apple’s valuation moment also shows how much the stock market is now shaped by a small group of very large technology companies.

Nvidia has recently been seen as one of the main winners from the AI boom. Apple is different. It is not usually described as the most exciting AI company, but it still has something very powerful: a huge customer base and a strong ecosystem.

I found that difference quite interesting. Nvidia’s rise is more directly linked to AI chips and data centres, while Apple’s strength comes more from brand, hardware, services and customer loyalty. Both can become extremely valuable, but for different reasons.

This made me realise that investors do not only reward the newest or fastest-growing trend. They also reward companies that look durable. Apple’s valuation suggests that many investors still trust the company’s ability to keep selling premium products and building services around them.

Finance Concept Explained: Market Capitalisation

The finance concept this week is market capitalisation, often shortened to market cap.

A simple way to think about it is:

Market capitalisation = share price × number of shares outstanding

So if Apple’s share price rises, its market cap rises too. But this does not mean Apple suddenly receives $5 trillion in cash. Market cap is the value the stock market is placing on Apple’s equity.

That difference is important. A company can have a huge market cap, but that does not mean it has that amount of money sitting in its bank account. It means investors are valuing ownership of the company at that level.

Market cap is useful because it helps compare the size of companies. But it does not tell the whole story. Two companies can have the same market cap but very different levels of debt, cash, profit margins and growth.

That is why finance professionals often look at enterprise value as well:

Enterprise value = market cap + debt − cash

Enterprise value tries to show the value of the whole business, not just the value of the shares. This matters because a company with a lot of debt is not the same as a company with a lot of cash, even if their market caps look similar.

So Apple’s $5 trillion moment is impressive, but the better finance question is: what future profits and cash flows are investors expecting Apple to produce?

My Take

I found this story useful because it made market capitalisation feel more real.

Before this, I understood the basic formula, but seeing it linked to Apple made me think more carefully about what the number actually means. Market cap shows how the stock market is valuing a company, but it does not automatically tell us whether that company is cheap, expensive or fairly valued.

To understand that properly, you have to look deeper at earnings, cash flow, debt, growth and the quality of the business.

For me, Apple’s $5 trillion valuation shows how much value the market can place on trust. Trust in the brand, trust in the ecosystem and trust that the company can keep turning its products and services into cash.

I’m still learning, so if I’ve missed something important, I’d genuinely appreciate being corrected.

5 July 2026: Microsoft’s £1.9bn AI Bet

Published 5 July 2026 · The Market Loop · 5 min read

Microsoft is already one of the biggest companies in the world, so at first this looked like just another AI headline. But the more I looked at it, the more interesting it became. This is not only about Microsoft spending money on AI. It is about whether companies can actually turn AI into something useful and profitable.

Investment $2.5bn
Approx. GBP £1.9bn
Specialists 6,000
Announced 2 July 2026

This Week in Markets

This week, Microsoft announced Microsoft Frontier Company, a new AI-focused business backed by $2.5 billion, which is roughly £1.9 billion.

The aim is to help large companies use AI properly inside their own businesses. That sounds simple, but it is actually where the story gets interesting. A lot of companies already want to use AI. The harder part is knowing what to use it for, how to connect it to their own data, and whether it actually improves the way the business works.

Microsoft said the new company will involve around 6,000 industry and engineering specialists working with customers. Some of the early names linked to the project include LSEG, Unilever, Land O’Lakes and Novo Nordisk.

This shows how the AI story is starting to move into a more serious stage. It is less about companies saying they are “using AI” and more about whether AI can actually help them save time, improve decisions or make their operations more efficient.

Deal / Company Story of the Week

Microsoft is already heavily involved in AI through Azure, Copilot and its partnership with OpenAI. But Microsoft Frontier Company seems to focus on a slightly different problem: implementation.

A company might want to use AI, but that does not mean it automatically knows how to use it well. It has to choose the right tools, connect them to its own systems, protect its data, and make sure the results are useful in a real business setting. That is probably much harder than just buying an AI subscription and hoping it helps.

That is where Microsoft is trying to position itself. It is not only selling software. It is trying to work more closely with companies and help them build AI into their normal operations.

The numbers make the scale clear. A $2.5 billion investment is not a small test. The 6,000 specialists also show that this is not only a technology story. It is also about consulting, operations, data, change management and long-term business strategy.

Microsoft is not just betting that AI will be powerful. It is betting that companies will pay to make AI work properly.

Finance Concept Explained: Return on Investment

The finance concept this week is return on investment, or ROI.

ROI is basically about asking whether an investment was worth it. A simple way to think about it is:

ROI = gain from investment ÷ cost of investment

So if a company spends money on AI, the key question is simple: what does it actually get back?

The answer could be faster work, lower costs, better customer service, stronger sales or better decision-making. But if the system is badly planned, the data is messy, or employees do not use it properly, the return could be much weaker than expected.

This matters because AI spending can sound impressive, but spending money does not automatically mean value has been created. Microsoft’s move focuses on the next stage of AI: helping businesses prove that the technology can create real value, not just interest.

My Take

I found this story useful because it made the AI boom feel more practical. A lot of AI news is about models, chips and huge valuations. But this story is more about what companies actually do with AI once they have access to it.

The main thing I learned is that saying a company “uses AI” does not really mean much by itself. The more important question is what the AI actually improves. Does it save time? Does it help staff work better? Does it reduce costs? Does it help the company grow?

That is where the finance side comes in. Companies still have to think about cost, risk and return. Even if the technology is exciting, the business case still has to make sense.

I also think this links quite well to investment banking and corporate finance. Companies are now making big strategic decisions around AI. Some will build their own systems, some will partner with companies like Microsoft, and some may buy smaller AI businesses to speed things up.

For me, Microsoft’s £1.9 billion AI bet shows that the next stage of AI may not just be about who has the best technology. It may be about who can actually turn that technology into something useful for businesses.

I’m still learning, so if I’ve missed something important, I’d genuinely appreciate being corrected.
